### 1. Choi & Ferrara (2024) — FACT-GPT: Fact-Checking Augmentation via Claim Matching with LLMs
**arXiv:2402.05904**

**What the paper does:**
Frames automated fact-checking as a three-class textual entailment task (ENTAILMENT / NEUTRAL / CONTRADICTION) between a social media post and a previously debunked claim. Collects 1,225 COVID-19 debunked claims from Google Fact Check Tools and PolitiFact, generates synthetic tweets using GPT-4 / GPT-3.5 / Llama-2-70b at temperature 1.0, and fine-tunes smaller models (GPT-3.5-Turbo, Llama-2-13b, Llama-2-7b) on the synthetic data. Best result: GPT-3.5-Turbo fine-tuned on GPT-4-generated data reaches 73% accuracy, F1_Ent = 0.83. Critical finding: all models struggle on CONTRADICTION labels, with F1_Con ≤ 0.46 across all model variants.

**How it influenced our work:**
- The name "FACT-GPT" for our project is directly borrowed from this paper; we append "v2" only to distinguish our system.
- We adopted the same three-class textual entailment formulation (ENTAILMENT → SUPPORTS, CONTRADICTION → REFUTES, NEUTRAL → NEUTRAL) as the core verdict mechanism, implemented using RoBERTa-large-MNLI rather than fine-tuned LLMs.
- Their reported weakness on CONTRADICTION labels (F1_Con ≤ 0.46) directly motivated our weighted multi-snippet aggregation design: instead of one model prediction on a single claim-document pair, we accumulate weighted votes across multiple independent evidence snippets, making refutation signals more robust than a single-pass verdict.
- Their pipeline structure — debunked claim corpus → evidence retrieval → entailment classification → verdict — is the conceptual skeleton of our pipeline, though we depart from the static corpus approach and replace it with live retrieval.
- Their finding that fine-tuning on higher-quality synthetic data (GPT-4 > GPT-3.5 > Llama-2-70b) directly improves downstream accuracy informed our decision to use Gemini-2.5-Flash (a higher-capability model) for claim extraction and query expansion rather than cheaper alternatives.

### 2. Alam et al. (2023) — Overview of CLEF-2023 CheckThat! Lab Task 1 on Check-Worthiness of Multimodal and Multigenre Content
**CEUR Workshop Proceedings, Vol-3497/paper-019**

**What the paper does:**
Organises Task 1 of CLEF-2023, which asks systems to determine whether a given text item (tweet, political debate sentence, or speech excerpt) is worth fact-checking. Two subtasks: 1A (multimodal: tweet + image) and 1B (multigenre: tweet / debate / speech) in Arabic, English, and Spanish. 15 teams, 155 official runs. Top findings: XLM-RoBERTa is the most widely used and effective backbone; OpenFact's GPT-3 fine-tune achieves F1 = 0.898 on English debate check-worthiness; aggressive preprocessing is consistently a top-team differentiator; low-resource languages (Arabic, Spanish) perform substantially worse than English. Inter-annotator agreement for check-worthiness (Cohen's Kappa = 0.49) reflects the inherent subjectivity of the task.

**How it influenced our work:**
- The two annotation questions used in CheckThat! Task 1B — "does it contain a verifiable factual claim?" and "is it harmful?" — are the direct basis of our Stage 4 claim-worthiness scoring function. We operationalise these as heuristic feature scores (factual verb presence, numeric content, message length, opinion-starter penalty) rather than a learned classifier, due to real-time latency constraints.
- Their finding that top-performing systems universally applied aggressive text normalisation before classification reinforced our decision to invest heavily in WhatsApp-specific preprocessing (forwarded-tag removal, alias resolution, emoji handling) before any NLP step.
- The observation that low-resource language tracks perform substantially worse than English directly predicts and explains our own Bengali (33% accuracy) and Hinglish (75% accuracy) results. We cite this as supporting context rather than presenting our low non-English accuracy as unexpected.
- The multigenre setting (tweets + debates + speeches in a single system) is the closest published analogue to our WhatsApp input, which similarly spans multiple registers and genres within one messaging channel.
- The OpenFact result (F1 = 0.898 via GPT-3 fine-tune on 7.7K labelled debate sentences) provides a meaningful upper bound for what a well-resourced check-worthiness classifier can achieve, against which our heuristic scorer is clearly weaker and does not claim to compete.

### 3. Thorne et al. (2018) — FEVER: a Large-scale Dataset for Fact Extraction and VERification
**NAACL 2018**

**What the paper does:**
Introduces FEVER, the foundational benchmark for evidence-grounded fact verification. Contains 185,445 claims generated by mutating Wikipedia sentences, each labelled SUPPORTED, REFUTED, or NOTENOUGHINFO, with sentence-level evidence annotations. Inter-annotator agreement: Fleiss κ = 0.6841. Baseline pipeline — TF-IDF document retrieval → sentence selection → decomposable attention NLI — achieves 31.87% label accuracy with evidence and 50.91% label-only. Key finding: the evidence retrieval stage, not the NLI model, is the primary bottleneck; a perfect retrieval oracle raises accuracy to over 76%.

**How it influenced our work:**
- FEVER establishes the three-stage pipeline (document retrieval → sentence selection → recognising textual entailment) that is the direct architectural blueprint of our Stages 5 and 6.
- Our Wikipedia-based live retrieval directly implements the FEVER document-retrieval stage; our use of RoBERTa-large-MNLI for stance classification follows the state-of-the-art approach developed on the FEVER leaderboard.
- The key departure from FEVER is temporal: FEVER uses a static Wikipedia snapshot, whereas we retrieve live evidence at inference time to avoid staleness on recent events.
- FEVER's finding that retrieval recall constrains overall accuracy more than NLI model quality is the theoretical justification for our primary latency optimisations (parallel retrieval, snippet-based RAG) — we optimise the retrieval stage precisely because it is the binding constraint.
- FEVER's three-class label set (SUPPORTED / REFUTED / NOTENOUGHINFO) maps directly to our verdict taxonomy (TRUE / FALSE / NOT VERIFIED).
- FEVER's scale (185K claims) is used throughout our paper as an explicit contrast to our 28-message evaluation, to honestly contextualise the statistical significance of our results.

### 4. Aly et al. (2021) — FEVEROUS: Fact Extraction and VERification Over Unstructured and Structured information
**NeurIPS 2021 Datasets and Benchmarks**

**What the paper does:**
Extends FEVER with FEVEROUS (87,026 claims), requiring evidence from both unstructured Wikipedia prose and structured table cells. Evidence can be sentences, table cells, or combinations of both. Baseline: a RoBERTa classifier pre-trained on NLI datasets achieves a FEVEROUS score of 0.18 when required to predict both correct evidence and the correct verdict jointly. Key finding: systems that combine structured and unstructured evidence substantially outperform text-only or table-only baselines; the joint evidence+verdict evaluation metric reveals that retrieving the right evidence is significantly harder than classifying the verdict given correct evidence.

**How it influenced our work:**
- FEVEROUS motivates our multi-source evidence architecture: our RAG pipeline collects Wikipedia article text (analogous to FEVEROUS unstructured sentences) and DuckDuckGo snippets (analogous to structured factual summaries), rather than relying on a single evidence type.
- The FEVEROUS finding that retrieval quality is the binding constraint — not the NLI classifier — is the second major theoretical justification (alongside the original FEVER analysis) for our decision to invest engineering effort in parallel retrieval and snippet caching rather than in upgrading the NLI model.
- The joint evidence+verdict metric in FEVEROUS is directly analogous to our system requirement: the output is only useful if we both retrieve relevant evidence and classify the stance correctly. A correct label with no evidence shown would not satisfy a WhatsApp user seeking a trustworthy verdict.

### 5. Lin et al. (2024) — CFEVER: A Chinese Dataset for Fact Extraction and VERification
**LREC-COLING 2024**

**What the paper does:**
Introduces CFEVER, the largest Chinese-language dataset for evidence-based fact verification (30,012 claims derived from Chinese Wikipedia), following FEVER's construction and labelling methodology exactly to allow cross-lingual comparison. Three-stage pipeline: BM25 document retrieval (recall 87.65%) → BERT sentence retrieval → RTE. Best system (BEVERS): 69.73% label accuracy, 64.80% FEVER Score. Fleiss κ = 0.7934, higher than FEVER's 0.6841. Key finding: multilingual FEVER-style verification is feasible but performance degrades substantially for claims requiring evidence from multiple Wikipedia pages.

**How it influenced our work:**
- CFEVER is the most directly analogous prior work to our multilingual setting because it demonstrates that the FEVER pipeline transfers to a non-English Wikipedia language with competitive performance, validating our design choice to use Wikipedia as the primary evidence source for translated non-English claims.
- CFEVER's finding that multi-page evidence degradation is the dominant failure mode directly predicts our Hindi and Bengali failure cases: NLLB-translated claims frequently retrieve topically unrelated English Wikipedia articles, mirroring the multi-page degradation CFEVER documents.
- CFEVER's scale (30K claims) is used alongside FEVER (185K) as an explicit baseline of evaluation scale against which our 28-message test set is honestly contextualised throughout the paper.
- The BEVERS system's 69.73% label accuracy provides a meaningful performance reference point for a well-engineered Wikipedia-retrieval pipeline on a non-English language, directly comparable to our 71% overall accuracy on English and 60–67% on non-English languages.

### 6. Ma et al. (2024) — EX-FEVER: A Dataset for Explainable Fact Verification
**arXiv:2310.09754**

**What the paper does:**
Introduces EX-FEVER (60,000+ claims requiring 2-hop or 3-hop Wikipedia reasoning), the first dataset for multi-hop *explainable* fact verification. Each claim is annotated with a veracity label (SUPPORTS / REFUTES / NOT ENOUGH INFO) *and* a human-written textual explanation of the full reasoning chain. Baseline: BERT verdict predictor achieves 73.34% on the test set; graph-based GEAR underperforms BERT, suggesting pattern-matching rather than genuine multi-hop reasoning is occurring. LLM finding: ChatGPT used as a "planner" (64.90% with gold evidence) outperforms ChatGPT used as a direct "actor" (58.69% few-shot), motivating hybrid architectures that separate planning from classification.

**How it influenced our work:**
- The multi-hop difficulty finding — that claims requiring evidence from two or more Wikipedia articles are substantially harder than single-hop claims — directly explains our failure on complex Hindi claims: NLLB-translated multi-clause sentences effectively require multi-hop reasoning that our single-hop retrieval cannot support.
- The explainability requirement in EX-FEVER is the primary motivation for our "correct figure extraction" module: when a claim is FALSE, we surface the highest-confidence refuting snippet as "What the evidence actually says," providing the user with an explanation rather than a bare verdict label. This is a single-hop, heuristic approximation of EX-FEVER's human-annotated multi-hop explanations.
- EX-FEVER's finding that LLMs perform better as planners than as direct predictors motivated our hybrid architecture: we use Gemini-2.5-Flash for high-level claim extraction and query expansion (planning) while delegating structured stance classification to RoBERTa-large-MNLI (classification). We do not claim the performance levels EX-FEVER reports; the architectural motivation is the relevant transfer.
- EX-FEVER's documentation that training on a related but distinct dataset (HOVER) generalises poorly to EX-FEVER directly parallels our ablation finding that enabling our static learned-verdict corpus reduced accuracy by ~8 pp, validating live retrieval over static corpora.
